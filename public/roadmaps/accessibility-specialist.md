## Reality check

Advocacy can be exhausting — convincing teams that accessibility matters is an ongoing battle. It's not always the most 'exciting' work. But making technology usable by everyone is one of the most impactful things you can do in tech.

## What a Accessibility Specialist actually does day to day

Accessibility specialists ensure that digital products are usable by people with disabilities. You advocate for inclusive design and implement WCAG standards. In practice the week looks less like continuous coding and more like a mix of building, reviewing, debugging and deciding. A typical day includes a short stand-up, two to four hours of focused build time, code review for teammates, and at least one conversation about scope or trade-offs. The people who progress fastest in this role are the ones who treat those conversations as part of the job rather than as an interruption to it.

- **Morning:** triage anything that broke overnight, then take the highest-leverage task rather than the easiest one.
- **Core hours:** deep work on the current increment — WCAG Guidelines, Screen Readers and HTML/CSS are the tools you will touch most.
- **Reviews:** reading other people's changes is the fastest way to learn a codebase and the fastest way to build trust.
- **Documentation:** a short written note about why a decision was made saves hours for the next person, often you in three months.
- **Learning:** the field moves; an hour a week on fundamentals beats a weekend binge every quarter.

## Accessibility Specialist interview preparation

Interview loops for this role typically run four to six stages. Expect a recruiter screen, a technical screen on fundamentals, a practical exercise or take-home, a deep-dive on your own projects, and a hiring-manager conversation about ownership and collaboration.

| Round | What is tested | Preparation that works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Screening | Motivation, communication, salary alignment | A 90-second summary of your work and a researched range |
| Technical fundamentals | WCAG Guidelines, Screen Readers and HTML/CSS | Daily reps for four weeks, explained out loud |
| Practical exercise | Code quality, tests, judgement about scope | Timebox it and document what you deliberately left out |
| Project deep-dive | Whether you actually built what your CV claims | Be able to justify every architectural choice you made |
| Hiring manager | Ownership, conflict, how you handle being wrong | Six STAR stories including one genuine failure |

- **User Research:** explain how you would debug a problem involving user research in production.
- **Assistive Technologies:** describe how assistive technologies fits into the systems you have built.
- **WCAG Guidelines:** walk through a trade-off you made using wcag guidelines and what you would do differently.
- **Screen Readers:** walk through a trade-off you made using screen readers and what you would do differently.
- **HTML/CSS:** compare two approaches within html/css and justify your default choice.
- **ARIA:** explain how you would debug a problem involving aria in production.
- **Automated Testing:** explain how you would debug a problem involving automated testing in production.

## Mistakes that slow people down

1. Collecting tutorials instead of finishing projects. Completion is the skill being trained.
2. Learning adjacent tools before the core ones. Get WCAG Guidelines and Screen Readers solid first.
3. Building only what the tutorial shows. The learning happens when something breaks and nobody has written the fix down.
4. Waiting until you feel ready to apply. Interview practice is a skill and it is trained by interviewing.
5. No public trail. A deployed link and a written case study is worth more than a private repository.
6. Ignoring fundamentals because the stack is modern. Complexity, data modelling and debugging are still what interviews test.

## Accessibility Specialist — frequently asked questions

### How long does it take to become a accessibility specialist?

6-10 months to job-ready for someone starting from scratch and studying 10–15 hours a week. People coming from an adjacent technical role usually move faster because they already understand how teams ship software.

### Is Accessibility Specialist a good career in 2027?

Demand is rated high. Strong — legal requirements (ADA, EAA) are expanding globally. Every company needs accessibility expertise.

### Do I need a degree to become a accessibility specialist?

No, though it still helps for visa-sponsored roles and large enterprises. What replaces it is evidence: deployed projects, a public code history, and the ability to explain your decisions clearly.

### How hard is it really?

Difficulty is moderate — roughly 3 out of 10. Advocacy can be exhausting — convincing teams that accessibility matters is an ongoing battle. It's not always the most 'exciting' work. But making technology usable by everyone is one of the most impactful things you can do in tech.

### What should I learn first?

Start with Fundamentals — specifically Disability Awareness, WCAG Guidelines and Semantic HTML. Everything later in the roadmap assumes this foundation.

### Can I switch to Accessibility Specialist from a non-technical background?

Yes, and thousands do each year. The realistic timeline is 6-10 months (entry) → 3-4 years (expert), the main risk is quitting in month four, and the strongest mitigation is a public build streak plus one person who expects progress from you weekly.

### Will AI replace accessibility specialists?

AI has changed the work rather than removed it. Code generation raised the floor, and the value moved toward design, debugging, evaluating correctness and understanding systems — the parts current models handle least reliably.
